Fake virus warnings and tech support calls

Part of the Wazirly scam library. Updated 28 November 2026.

Short answer: a warning with a phone number to call is a scam. Microsoft says its error and warning messages never include a phone number (Microsoft), and the US Federal Trade Commission says real security warnings never ask you to call a number (FTC). The FBI counted $2.1 billion lost to tech and customer support scams in the US in 2025 (FBI IC3 2025 report).

What the message looks like

Example of a scam pop-up. Written for this page.

WARNING: Your device is infected with 3 viruses. Your bank details are at risk. Call support now. Do not close this window.

Example of a scam call. Written for this page.

Hello, I'm calling from the technical department. Your device has been sending us error reports. I'll help you fix it, please install the app I'm sending you.

Example of the next step. Written for this page.

To refund your support fee we need you to log in to your online banking while I'm connected.

How it unfolds, step by step

  1. The opener. A pop-up in your browser, a call or a text saying your device is infected or your account has been hacked.
  2. Building trust. The "technician" shows you proof on your screen: error lists, warnings, scans.
  3. Moving you to another app. They ask you to install a remote access app, so they can see and control your screen.
  4. The ask. Pay for the fix by gift card, wire or crypto (FTC), or log in to your bank so they can check it.
  5. The pressure. Don't close the window, don't turn it off, your money is at risk right now.
  6. The hand-off. In the version the FBI calls the Phantom Hacker scam, a second caller from your bank and then a third from a government agency tell you to move your money to protect it (FBI).
  7. The exit. The money is gone, and the remote access app may still be on your device.

The signs on your screen

  • A phone number inside a warning.
  • A call about a virus that you didn't ask for. Microsoft says it doesn't make unsolicited support calls.
  • A request to install an app so someone can see your screen.
  • Payment by gift card, wire or crypto.
  • Being told not to close the window or tell anyone.

What to do now

Don't call the number, and don't install anything. Close the browser tab. If it won't close, close the browser app or restart the phone. If someone called you, hang up.

If you already paid or let them in

Remove the app they asked you to install, and change the passwords they saw or that you typed, from another device if you can. Microsoft suggests resetting the device if the scammer had remote access (Microsoft). Then call your bank on the number on your card. If you paid by gift card, call the card's company. Then report it where you live:

  • US: report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and at ic3.gov if it happened online.
  • UK: call 159 to reach your own bank (Stop Scams UK). Report it to Report Fraud, online or on 0300 123 2040, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (it replaced Action Fraud in December 2025), or to Police Scotland on 101.
  • Canada: report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or 1-888-495-8501, and to your local police.
  • Australia: report it to Scamwatch, and to ReportCyber if you lost money online. IDCARE helps for free on 1800 595 160 if you shared personal details.

Every number and website, country by country, is on Report a scam.

Questions

Is the virus warning on my phone real?

If it shows a phone number to call, no. Real security warnings never ask you to call a number. Close the browser tab and don't call.

Does Microsoft call people about viruses?

No. Microsoft says it doesn't make unsolicited calls to offer technical support. The FTC says real tech companies won't call, email or text you to say there's a problem with your computer.

I let them into my computer or phone. What now?

Remove the app they asked you to install, change your passwords from another device, and call your bank on the number on your card. If odd things keep happening, reset the device.

What is the Phantom Hacker scam?

The FBI's name for a three-step scam: a fake tech support call, then a fake bank, then a fake government agency, each telling you to move your money to protect it.
