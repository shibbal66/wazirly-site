Recovery scams: the second scam after the first

Part of the Wazirly scam library. Updated 28 November 2026.

Short answer: if someone contacts you offering to get back money you lost, and wants a fee first, it's a scam. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center says it will never contact victims directly or ask for payment to recover lost money (FBI IC3). People reported losing about $1.4 billion to recovery scams to the FBI in 2025 (FBI IC3 2025 report).

What the message looks like

Example of a scam message. Written for this page.

We are a licensed fund recovery firm. Our records show you lost money to a crypto platform. We have traced your funds and can return them for a 10% fee.

Example of a scam call script. Written for this page.

This is an agent from the cybercrime unit. We have recovered $48,000 linked to your case. To release it, you must first pay a processing tax.

Example of a social media comment. Written for this page.

I got all my money back thanks to this recovery expert, message them on Telegram!

How it unfolds, step by step

  1. The opener. A message, call or email soon after your loss. In one study of 26 investment-scam victims, more than half were targeted again, 2 weeks to 3 months later (Oak and others, 2025). Scammers buy lists of people who've already paid scammers (FTC).
  2. Building trust. An official-sounding name: the FBI, IC3, the police, a law firm, a consumer group. Case numbers, websites, a portal to log in to (FTC; Oak and others).
  3. The bait. Your money has been found.
  4. The ask. A fee first, called a retainer, a processing fee or a tax, plus your ID and bank statements.
  5. The pressure. Pay before a deadline or the funds go back.
  6. The exit. Another fee, then silence.

The signs on your screen

  • They contacted you, not the other way round.
  • A fee before you get anything back.
  • A claim to be IC3 or the FBI. IC3 never contacts victims directly by phone, email or social media.
  • Payment by crypto, gift card or wire.
  • A promise that your money will definitely come back.

What to do now

Never pay to get money back. Real police and government agencies don't charge victims. Talk only to your own bank and the police, using numbers you look up yourself.

If you already paid

Stop paying, and call your bank on the number on your card. Then report it where you live:

  • US: report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and at ic3.gov if it happened online. If you're 60 or over, the Justice Department's elder fraud hotline is 1-833-372-8311 (FBI IC3).
  • UK: call 159 to reach your own bank (Stop Scams UK). Report it to Report Fraud, online or on 0300 123 2040, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (it replaced Action Fraud in December 2025), or to Police Scotland on 101. If you sent a bank transfer, your bank must usually pay you back, up to £85,000, if you report it within 13 months; a £100 excess may apply (Payment Systems Regulator).
  • Canada: report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or 1-888-495-8501, and to your local police.
  • Australia: report it to Scamwatch, and to ReportCyber if you lost money online. IDCARE helps for free on 1800 595 160 if you shared personal details.

Every number and website, country by country, is on Report a scam.

Questions

Can someone really recover money lost to a scam?

Sometimes your bank or the police can, which is why you should report quickly. In 2025 the FBI's Recovery Asset Team froze $146.6 million after quick reports at ic3.gov. But no one legitimate charges you a fee upfront to do it.

The FBI contacted me about my case. Is that real?

Be very careful. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center says it never contacts people directly by phone, email or social media, and never asks for payment to recover lost money.

Is a crypto recovery service legit?

If it contacted you first, or wants a fee before it recovers anything, treat it as a scam. The FTC says anyone who asks for an upfront fee to get your money back is a scammer.

Why am I being targeted again?

Because scammers buy and share lists of people who've already paid a scammer. Being targeted again isn't your fault, and it's a reason to say no to every recovery offer.
