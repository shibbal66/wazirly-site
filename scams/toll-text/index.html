Unpaid toll text: is it a scam?

Part of the Wazirly scam library. Updated 28 November 2026.

Short answer: almost always, yes. A text that says you owe a toll and gives you a link to pay is a common scam in the US and Australia. The US Federal Trade Commission listed fake toll notices among the five most-reported text scams of 2024 (FTC), and the FBI has warned about them since 2024 (FBI IC3). They copy real names, from E-ZPass, SunPass and FasTrak in the US to Linkt in Australia. Don't tap the link.

What the message looks like

Example of a scam text, US. Written for this page.

Toll Services: You have an unpaid toll balance of $6.99. Pay by tomorrow to avoid a $50 late fee and a report to the DMV: [link]

Example of a scam text, Australia. Written for this page.

Linkt: Your toll account is overdue. Pay within 12 hours to avoid a fine: [link]

Example of a scam text. Written for this page.

FINAL NOTICE: Our records show an outstanding toll for your vehicle. Settle it today to keep your licence: [link]

How it unfolds, step by step

  1. The opener. A text from a number you don't know says you owe a small toll. The amount is small on purpose: it's easy to pay without thinking.
  2. Building trust. This scam skips it. It borrows trust from a real toll name instead, and the link goes to a copy of that toll service's site. The FBI found the fake sites copy each state's toll service name, and the phone numbers change from state to state (FBI IC3).
  3. The pressure. A deadline (today, within 12 hours) and a threat: a late fee, a fine, a report to the DMV.
  4. The ask. The page asks for your card number, and often your name, address and date of birth.
  5. The second ask. Many of these sites then say your bank will send a one-time code to verify the payment. On these sites, that code really approves your card being added to a phone wallet on the criminal's phone (KrebsOnSecurity).
  6. The exit. Nothing happens for days. Researchers found criminals often wait 7 to 10 days before using the cards they've added. So paying and hearing nothing back is not a good sign.

The signs on your screen

  • A toll notice by text, with a link. Linkt says it will never ask you to pay through a link in a text (Linkt).
  • A deadline and a threat in the same message.
  • A web address that isn't the toll service's own address, often with extra words or odd endings.
  • A page that asks for a code your bank just sent you. On a lookalike site, that code can add your card to a scammer's phone wallet.
  • You don't remember driving on a toll road at all.

What to do now

Don't tap the link. If you think you might really owe a toll, check by typing the toll service's own website or opening its app yourself, or call the number on your account or statement, not the one in the text (FTC).

Then report it and delete it. In the US, use your phone's Report junk option or forward the text to 7726 (SPAM), and the FBI takes toll-text reports at ic3.gov with the phone number and website from the text (FBI IC3). In the UK and Canada, forward scam texts to 7726 too. In Australia, report it to Scamwatch.

If you already paid

Call your bank or card company now, on the number on the back of your card, and ask them to cancel the card. If you typed in a code from your bank, tell them: it may have added your card to someone else's phone wallet. Then report it where you live:

  • US: report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and at ic3.gov if it happened online. If you gave your date of birth or Social Security number, IdentityTheft.gov makes a recovery plan (FTC).
  • UK: call 159 to reach your own bank (Stop Scams UK). Report it to Report Fraud, online or on 0300 123 2040, in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (it replaced Action Fraud in December 2025), or to Police Scotland on 101.
  • Canada: report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or 1-888-495-8501, and to your local police.
  • Australia: report it to Scamwatch, and to ReportCyber if you lost money online. IDCARE helps for free on 1800 595 160 if you shared personal details.

Every number and website, country by country, is on Report a scam.

Questions

Is the unpaid toll text real?

Almost never. The FTC calls these texts probably a scam and says to check with the toll agency using a website or phone number you know is real, not the link in the text. Linkt in Australia says it never asks for payment through a link in a text.

What happens if I clicked the link?

If you only opened the page and typed nothing, close it, delete the text and don't go back. If you typed your card number, call your bank on the number on your card now and ask them to cancel it. If you also typed a code, tell them: it may have added your card to a scammer's phone wallet.

Why do I get toll texts when I don't drive?

Because they go out in bulk, to drivers and non-drivers alike. Google's lawsuit against one group behind these texts says it harmed more than a million victims in 120 countries.

How do I report a toll text?

In the US, forward it to 7726 (SPAM), then report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov or ic3.gov. In the UK and Canada, forward it to 7726. In Australia, report it to Scamwatch. Then delete it.
