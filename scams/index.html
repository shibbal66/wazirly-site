How scams work, step by step

The Wazirly scam library, for the US, the UK, Canada and Australia. Updated 28 November 2026.

Most scams follow the same path. Knowing the path helps you see where a chat is heading before it asks for money. Each guide below shows a scam stage by stage, the signs on your screen, the one thing to do now, and where to report it in your country if you've already paid.

The path most scams follow

  1. The opener. A text, call, pop-up or message you didn't expect: a toll, a parcel, a wrong number, a job, your bank.
  2. Building trust. A friendly chat, an official name, a real-looking website, or weeks of daily messages.
  3. Moving you to another app. From texts, a marketplace or a dating app to WhatsApp or Telegram, away from the first platform's own protections.
  4. The bait. A refund, a profit, an easy job, a problem only they can fix.
  5. The ask. Money, a code, your card details, a link to sign in, or an app to install.
  6. The pressure. A deadline, a threat, guilt, and a request to tell no one.
  7. The exit. They disappear. Sometimes a recovery scam follows, offering to get the money back for a fee.

Short scams, like a toll text, go from the opener to the ask in one message. Long ones can take months: in interviews with 26 victims of investment scams that began with a wrong-number text, a dating app or social media, the friendly stage lasted 3 to 11 months (Oak and others, 2025). Google describes the same thing: conversational scams begin innocently and turn dangerous mid-conversation (Google).

The guides

  • Unpaid toll texts

    A small toll, a deadline and a link. How the fake toll notice gets your card, and then the code that unlocks it.

  • Parcel delivery texts

    Your parcel is held until you pay a small fee. What USPS, Canada Post and Australia Post say they never do.

  • "Hi Mum, new number" texts

    A message from your child on a new number, then a bill they can't pay. Also known as Hi Mom and Hi Dad.

  • "Wrong number" texts

    A friendly stranger who keeps chatting, then a trading tip. How pig butchering works, stage by stage.

  • Task and remote job scams

    Easy money for liking videos or rating apps, until you have to deposit your own money to get paid.

  • Bank "safe account" calls

    Your bank's fraud team says your money is at risk and must be moved. It's the scam, not the fix.

  • IRS, HMRC, CRA and ATO scams

    Texts and calls that copy the tax office or Social Security. What each agency says it never does.

  • "I sent you a code by mistake"

    A friend on WhatsApp asks for the six-digit code you just got. Sharing it hands over your account.

  • Marketplace buyer and seller scams

    Buyers who want a code or send a fake payment, and sellers who want a deposit first.

  • Fake virus pop-ups and tech support calls

    A pop-up with a phone number, or a call from support. What happens after you call, and the hand-off to a fake bank.

  • "We can get your money back"

    After a scam, someone offers to recover your money for a fee, or says they're the FBI. It's the second scam.

Questions

What is the most common scam text?

In the US, fake parcel delivery texts were the most reported text scam of 2024, according to the FTC; its top five also included fake jobs, fake fraud alerts, unpaid tolls and wrong-number texts. In a UK study of reports to 7726, wrong-number openers were the most common.

How can I tell if a message is a scam?

Look for the ask: money, a code, a link to pay or sign in, or an app to install, usually with a deadline. Then check with the company or person through a route you already trust, not anything in the message.

Where do I report a scam?

In the US, ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov. In the UK, forward texts to 7726 and report to Report Fraud, or Police Scotland on 101. In Canada, the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. In Australia, Scamwatch and ReportCyber. Call your bank first if you paid.
